Case title: The Carval Dossier  
Location: Private residence of Cassandra “Cassie” Carval — two-story family house in a mountain town in Benguet  
Key dates: December 19–20, 2025  
Victim: Cassandra “Cassie” Carval (age 42\) — former DPWH Undersecretary; investigative civic researcher who compiled the “Carval dossier,” a hard-copy set of documents exposing anomalies in flood-control projects and cross-department corruption (DPWH, procurement, local permitting, and implicated national figures). Cassie keeps a physical set of the dossier in a heavy safe in her study.

High-level synopsis  
Cassie invited five trusted colleagues—senior officials and political figures—to her house on December 19, 2025 to review sensitive material. They spent the evening discussing a dossier that names players across DPWH, the Senate, and other agencies. In the morning of December 20, Cassie was found stabbed in the main hall; the hard copy dossier from her safe is missing. Everyone who attended is now a primary suspect. The following narrative lays out the house, characters (with roles revised), motives tied to the DPWH flood-control anomalies, the timeline with timestamps, the physical clues, and the interviews. At the end is a full reveal — who did it, what each person actually did that night, the safe pin, and the hidden phone/passphrase detail.

Summary of real sequence (night reconstructed)

1. Cassie locked the dossier in the study safe at 23:38 and went upstairs. Several people saw or heard her do this. The safe was closed and, as recorded, Cassie announced it was locked.  
2. Miguel Reyes had been anxious all evening. Earlier he had scribbled digits (2-7-6) while reviewing a legal note but these digits were not necessarily the entire code — only three digits he had noticed on some paperwork. Miguel spent time in the kitchen after 23:40 clearing dishes and thinking. He made a short, cryptic message around 00:12 to an unknown contact.  
3. At 00:20 the house experienced a brief power flicker and StudyCam pixelation; Miguel used that brief glitch window (00:20–00:23) to slip from the kitchen toward the study area. He had two advantages:  
   * He had seen and copied the three digits (2-7-6) earlier (napkin and pad).  
   * He was willing to try guessed combinations during a short window of low camera clarity.  
4. Miguel attempted to open the safe during the glitch. He used the 2-7-6 sequence as part of a brute force trial (he guessed probable trailing digits based on visible indentations and a pattern he inferred from a legal memo). He succeeded in opening the safe while the StudyCam was degraded or briefly ambiguous. Evidence tying Miguel to the safe:  
   * The circled digits on his pad and napkin (2-7-6).  
   * A smudged partial print on the safe rim consistent with his hand pressure (lab later matches the partial to Miguel).  
   * A small smear on the safe keypad consistent with his touch pattern.  
5. Finding the dossier, Miguel intended to remove copies quickly or copy key pages; Cassie returned to the study unexpectedly (she checked something upstairs or returned to retrieve a small item). A confrontation followed between Cassie and Miguel in the study or adjacent area. Words escalated into a short physical struggle.  
6. In the chaos, Miguel grabbed the largest available knife (the chef’s knife from the kitchen block) and, in a panicked, violent act, stabbed Cassie. The attack occurred in the study/main hall area; wound indicates a large blade; there were signs of a struggle and blood smears consistent with this altercation.  
7. Panicked, Miguel wrapped the knife in cloth and carried it out to the greenhouse and concealed it under a loose tile, removing it from the immediate scene. He then took the dossier (or the critical, most incriminating sections) and moved through the house trying to decide how to dispose of or hide the documents.  
8. Miguel, fearing he would be implicated, attempted to stage confusion:  
   * He placed a decoy page in Senator Liza’s room (or accidentally dropped a page there), which Liza later found and, panicked by the legal and political danger, burned the pages in her trash. Senator Liza did not kill Cassie; she burned the pages out of fear (a reactive, not conspiratorial act).  
   * Miguel also left traces that pointed to others (a torn cuff planted near the back path; he knew movements and could nudge suspicion toward Teresa), but this was sloppiness under stress, not a coordinated frame.  
9. Miguel later hid a small set of pages in a different location (or attempted to pass them to an outside contact), but he did not complete the upload. He believed that with the dossier removed he had a bargaining chip. He also tried to remove obvious incriminating residues (but traces remained: a small napkin with hemoglobin under his bed; a crumpled napkin with “2-7-6” in the kitchen trash).  
10. The next morning Jose found Cassie’s body at 06:22 and summoned authorities. Miguel attempted to maintain his original story (kitchen cleaning, sleeping) but fragments of evidence (napkin, partial print on safe rim, hemoglobin trace under bed) and his phone activity made him the highest-probability actor when investigators combined likelihoods.

* Miguel removed some physical pages and tried to control or destroy evidence, but he did not have the driver’s passphrase needed for Cassie’s phone, which would have instantly published the dossiers.

He remains loyal and does not provide the passphrase to Miguel — in effect the fail-safe remains intact.

Why the friend who burned pages is not the killer

* Senator Liza burned partial pages she discovered in panic; forensic analysis shows the timing is after Miguel had removed and concealed most critical pages. Liza’s fingerprints and movements do not match the struggle nor the partial prints on the safe; she is frightened and culpable for destroying evidence but is not the assailant. Her act is a panic concealment, not homicide.

Closing note on motives and morals (epilogue type)

* Miguel’s action was motivated by preservation of client networks and status — he chose a rapid, unlawful, and violent attempt to suppress data. The case underlines the story’s moral tension between sense (accountability, policy transparency) and sensibility (personal fear, panic, and human reactions): Cassie embodied the balance of both; her friends were torn between protecting institutions, careers, and conscience — actions afterwards (burning pages, lying) were often emotional reactions rather than premeditated murder by those actors.
